# Language-identification benchmarks still grade their own homework

Within the limits of this review, **no benchmark exists that is at once held out from tool training data, labelled per file by humans, sampled from a defined population, made of whole files and focused on ambiguous extensions**; every candidate fails at least two of those five tests. The review maps **83 benchmarks and datasets** from 2002 to 2026 and finds that nearly all of them take their labels from the file extension, the repository's GitHub language, a Stack Overflow tag or GitHub Linguist itself, which are the signals the tools under test already use. Label reliability is measured in exactly one programming-language identification (PLI) dataset: PLangRec's parser check rejected **6.1%** of extension-labelled GitHub files ([PLangRec](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167739X24006046)). Leakage controls are rare: four works split by repository, one uses a temporal hold-out, and Linguist's `samples/` directory, the de facto test set, is the training data of Linguist, go-enry and Hyperpolyglot ([go-enry classifier](https://github.com/go-enry/go-enry/blob/71719e4bdde528496011d030354a97b7f3ed4ff6/classifier.go)). Only one peer-reviewed study evaluates a generative LLM on PLI, on a public 2017 Stack Overflow dump and without any contamination control ([SCC-GPT](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7390/12/13/2128)), and only one blog post compares tools with and without file names on the same files ([GitHub blog](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/machine-learning/c-or-java-typescript-or-javascript-machine-learning-based-classification-of-programming-languages/)). The methods a sound benchmark needs do exist, but outside PLI: deduplication and time-segmented evaluation in code-model research, validated labels and abstention scoring in digital forensics, design-weighted estimation in survey sampling. A `bench-heldout` sampled from Software Heritage, stratified on ambiguous extensions and adjudicated by humans would therefore be new. That claim is bounded by an incomplete search, described next.

## Limits of this review: the search was cut short and most numbers came through a summarising reader

**All seven research strands ran out of web-search budget part-way** (a cap of 200 calls shared across the session), so each strand stopped before its planned queries were done. **Google Scholar, IEEE Xplore, the ACM Digital Library, the DBLP and arXiv search interfaces were not reachable, and Semantic Scholar, OpenAlex and Crossref throttled list and keyword queries** for most of the session; discovery relied on general web search, citation chasing from a few seeds and direct fetches of known URLs. **Most paper content was read through a fetch tool that returns a model-written extraction of the page, not the raw PDF.** Quoted strings and numbers are therefore "as extracted" and need re-checking against the PDFs before publication; one researcher caught the tool inventing a bibliographic entry (it attributed the seed paper's PDF to "Gerhold & Zaytsev, pp. 152-156, DOI 10.1109/SANER.2016.90"), which is not used anywhere in this report. The exception is tool behaviour: most facts about Linguist, go-enry, Hyperpolyglot, Pygments, Guesslang, Magika, cloc, tokei, scc and ohcount were read from repository clones at pinned commits and are reproducible. **Coverage is solid for roughly 2002 to 2021 and incomplete for 2022 to 2026**, because forward snowballing was possible from only a few papers. **Every "not found" in this report means "not found within those limits"**, not "does not exist".

## Deliverable 2 — Synthesis: the field measures agreement with inherited labels, on balanced sets of mainstream languages

### What is measured, and how

PLI is evaluated on four units: whole files, almost always from GitHub; Stack Overflow snippets; single lines, in one work; and byte fragments in the forensic literature. The typical study builds its own set, balances it per class, filters files by size and reports accuracy or F1 over a closed label set of 3 to 149 classes. On such sets whole-file papers report **97% to 99%**: 97% on 60 classes ([Gilda, abstract](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1109/JCSSE.2017.8025917?select=id,title,publication_year,authorships,biblio,primary_location,cited_by_count,abstract_inverted_index)), F 0.99 on 29 classes ([Zevin & Holzem](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.07638)), 99% F1 on 113 content types ([Magika](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.13768)). The one test that keeps the natural class distribution of popular repositories, over 133 extension classes, reports **85% accuracy and a macro-F1 of 0.71** ([Del Bonifro et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.08508); the macro figure is among those the notes disagree on). Snippet work plateaus near **88%** across four transformer papers on one Stack Overflow corpus ([Ma et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.05544v4)), which the researchers read as a ceiling set by tag noise and ambiguous snippets; no paper measures that ceiling.

Run on content alone, deployed tools score far lower. In the only head-to-head found, Pygments' `guess_lexer` reaches 10% to 37% and highlight.js 36% to 56% across three test sets ([language-detection.el README](https://github.com/andreasjansson/language-detection.el)). Linguist's own leave-one-out script tolerates **744 errors** when every sample is classified from content against all languages, against 14 when candidates are first narrowed by extension ([script/cross-validation](https://github.com/github-linguist/linguist/blob/5fbdfcb8133be2bed88bf3ce62b2335f50474525/script/cross-validation)); one researcher's arithmetic puts the content-only error at 23% or more if that ceiling is tight, a figure Linguist does not publish. One tool also moves widely with the test distribution: Guesslang is reported at 93.45% by its author ([docs](https://guesslang.readthedocs.io/en/latest/)), 77% F1 on Magika's supported text types, 84.67% on 385 hand-checked Stack Overflow answers ([Zolduoarrati et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.03670)) and, in an extraction of PLangRec's table that needs re-checking, 95.11% on whole files but 24.57% on single lines. Papers seldom run each other's tools. The usual comparison is a related-work table that lines up accuracies obtained on different datasets with 10 to 60 labels, and those tables copy each other's errors, as the "19 languages, 97.5%" entry for the seed paper shows.

### Four pitfalls recur

**Leakage.** It takes three forms. The first is evaluation on training data: Linguist's `samples/` trains Linguist's classifier and those of go-enry and Hyperpolyglot; Hyperpolyglot's only quantified check asserts accuracy 1.0 on its copy of the samples ([lib.rs](https://github.com/monkslc/hyperpolyglot/blob/a55a3b58eaed09b4314ef93d78e50a80cfec36f4/src/lib.rs#L385-L430)), go-enry's README benchmark, which measures speed only, runs on them, and Linguist's unit test trains and tests on the same files ([test_classifier.rb](https://github.com/github-linguist/linguist/blob/5fbdfcb8133be2bed88bf3ce62b2335f50474525/test/test_classifier.rb)). Because heuristics are edited until those samples pass, the directory is, in the researchers' reading, a development set for the rule layer too. The second form is a random split without deduplication or source separation: the SCC corpus, Magika's 26.5 M files, PLangRec's lines and FFT-75's blocks are all shuffled, although 70% of 428 M GitHub files in one study are clones ([DéjàVu](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/3133908)). The third concerns learned tools and LLMs, for which labels leak as well as content: The Stack v2 labels 3.28 B files with go-enry ([StarCoder2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.19173)), so a model trained on it has seen Linguist's decisions as metadata. The requester's observation, near-perfect scores on `samples/` and lower ones on newer files, fits this pattern. The academic whole-file papers that could be read do not themselves report using `samples/` as a test set; the leak lives in tools and engineering benchmarks, and Reyes et al. is the one paper still to check.

**Label noise.** Extension labels fail measurably wherever someone has looked. A parser check rejected 6.1% of 9.07 M extension-labelled files ([PLangRec](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167739X24006046)); a compiler-based audit reports 34.7% of sampled "C" files in The Stack to be Objective-C ([CompCodeVet](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.06505)); volunteer annotators excluded 36 of 300 extensions from The Stack v1 and 130 of about 1,000 from v2 as not being human-written code of the labelled language ([StarCoder](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.06161), [StarCoder2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.19173)); and 25 of 41 projects labelled TypeScript in a well-known study contained none, because `.ts` files were XML translations ([Berger et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1901.10220)). Stack Overflow tags and Linguist output "are often not consistent" on snippets ([Dietrich et al., abstract](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1109/msr.2019.00041?select=id,title,publication_year,authorships,biblio,primary_location,cited_by_count,abstract_inverted_index)). Yet no study found draws a random sample of whole files, has humans name the language and reports agreement.

**Circular, extension-derived labels.** The label is usually produced by the kind of signal under test. Guesslang's training labels come from an extension table "based on" Linguist's `languages.yml`, with the repository language as tie-breaker ([guesslangtools](https://github.com/yoeo/guesslangtools/blob/f7fd19d23b871d5a17ad25683d4aea3b1aa6c0f6/guesslangtools/workflow/source_files.py#L197-L217)); OctoLingua's come from Linguist's repository classification; Del Bonifro et al.'s image set and The Stack v2 use Linguist or go-enry outright, the former with the caveat that results "should be diminished by Linguist's performances" ([PeerJ](https://peerj.com/articles/cs-631)). The digital-preservation community named the problem in 2011: "in most large data sets this information will be derived from the very same tools that we are trying to test" ([van der Knijff](https://bitsgalore.org/2011/09/21/evaluation-identification-tools-first-results-scape)). Extension labels are weakest exactly where identifiers differ: **173 of Linguist's 1,489 extensions (11.6%) are claimed by two to twelve languages** (the researchers' count on [languages.yml](https://github.com/github-linguist/linguist/blob/5fbdfcb8133be2bed88bf3ce62b2335f50474525/lib/linguist/languages.yml)), and The Stack v1 resolved such collisions by dropping `.m`, `.v` and `.inc` or forcing `.h` to C and `.pl` to Perl ([BigCode notebook](https://github.com/bigcode-project/bigcode-dataset/blob/bebec929edd826f19b5fa3538f22d18d5b50da4b/language_selection/the-stack-11-language-selection.ipynb)).

**Label-set mismatch.** Linguist has 836 entries, Guesslang 54, Magika 113 in its paper and 214 in its current model, the line counters 333 to 390. The same file is MATLAB for Linguist, Octave for Pygments and matlab for Magika; a `.service` file is INI or Systemd; SQL is "data" in Linguist while its dialects are "programming". "Not code" means at least five things in practice (prose, data or configuration, generated, vendored, binary). Only Magika scores each baseline twice, on the types the tool supports and on all types, and the gap is large: Guesslang falls from 77% to 52% F1 on text types ([Magika](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.13768)). PLangRec instead restricts the comparison to 15 shared languages. No paper was found that proposes a mapping method, family-level scoring or open-set evaluation for PLI. The file name is a related blind spot: only the OctoLingua post tests the same files untouched, without extensions and with scrambled ones, and cloc, tokei, scc and, in practice, Hyperpolyglot cannot answer without a name at all, so a content-only column is "not applicable" for them, not zero.

### Surveys, the neighbouring field and LLMs: three thin literatures

One survey of machine-learning PLI exists: Verma et al. in IEEE Access (2025). Only its abstract was read; it says the paper covers identification in text, images and video and analyses Linguist, Pygments, highlight.js, Ace, Google Code Prettify, SourcererCC, Guesslang and SonarLint ([record](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1109/ACCESS.2025.3538108?select=id,title,publication_year,authorships,biblio,primary_location,cited_by_count,abstract_inverted_index)). Whether it inventories benchmarks is unknown, and no systematic mapping of PLI benchmarks was found. File-type identification has a taxonomy of fragment-classification techniques ([Poisel et al.](https://eudl.eu/pdf/10.1007/978-3-319-14289-0_6)), position papers ([Roussev & Garfinkel](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA549423.pdf)), a section of a 2020 systematisation ([Du et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.01987)) and a 2025 book chapter read by abstract only ([Mary & Sreeja](https://archives.christuniversity.in/items/show/27436)); no recent systematic review was located. Its criticisms transfer directly. The standard corpus warns that the extension is only suggestive of the type ([Govdocs1](https://digitalcorpora.org/corpora/files/)); container formats make a single label wrong for part of the data; corpora are private or incomparable; in-distribution test sets hide domain shift; and classifiers should be allowed to answer "unable to classify". NapierOne shows the stronger practice of validating each file by magic number and by opening it in its native application ([arXiv:2201.08154](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.08154)). The forensic sources do not name leakage between fragments of one file as a pitfall in so many words; that reading of FFT-75's block-level shuffle is the researchers'.

LLMs have been evaluated on PLI once. SCC-GPT prompts text-davinci-003, zero-shot, to choose among 19 listed languages for Stack Overflow snippets from a July 2017 dump and reports median F1 gains of "+6% to +31%" over earlier classifiers; on contamination the paper notes only that the model changed during the study ([SCC-GPT](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7390/12/13/2128)). A 2026 paper known by abstract only lists StarCoder and CodeLlama among its baselines for tasks that include PLI ([CodeLite](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1145/3839361?select=id,title,publication_year,authorships,biblio,primary_location,cited_by_count,abstract_inverted_index)). The code-benchmark suites that were checked, CodeXGLUE, xCodeEval and CodeScope, contain no PLI task ([CodeXGLUE](https://github.com/microsoft/CodeXGLUE)). LLMs otherwise appear on the other side of the task: detectors are used to measure the language of LLM output ([Moumoula et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13620v2)), an LLM judge verifies training labels on one model card, and fence tags emitted by LLMs are taken as labels without validation ([CodeChat](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.10402)). One 2026 repository-classification paper drops programming languages as targets because they are "very easy classes to guess (especially if a classifier can access file names)" ([DRAGON](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.09071v1)), which is the assumption a held-out benchmark tests.

**Is there a held-out, human-labelled, population-sampled benchmark of whole files with ambiguous extensions? No, not within the limits of this review.** Every candidate fails at least two of the five properties.

Other absences follow from the same search. No benchmark labels spans or allows several languages per file; multi-language items are removed by construction ([SCC](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1809.07945)) or given a container label. No evaluation found covers JCL, PL/I, RPG or MUMPS as test classes, or reports accuracy on Fortran source forms, assembly syntaxes, or BASIC, Lisp and shell dialects. Open-set behaviour is untested with real unknowns: Magika's TXT and UNKNOWN classes are random characters and random bytes. No PLI study uses a temporal cut for learned tools, and the one LLM study evaluates a model on data that, in the researchers' judgement, it almost certainly saw. ## Deliverable 3 — Recommendations for a held-out benchmark, each tied to the work it comes from

What each source reports is stated first; the transfer to PLI is the researchers' interpretation unless the source is itself about PLI.

### Independence from training sets

**Treat Linguist's `samples/` as contaminated and remove it, and its near-duplicates, from the test set, then report the residual overlap.** Allamanis shows that duplicates between training and test inflate metrics "by up to 100%" and recommends deduplicating at collection time ([Allamanis 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.06469)); Hernández López et al. report inter-dataset overlap as a percentage, up to 23% in their cases ([arXiv:2401.07930](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.07930)); Kapoor & Narayanan list duplicates as a leakage type ([arXiv:2207.07048](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.07048)). For Linguist-family tools the exclusion should extend, ideally, to repositories cited in heuristics pull requests. **Deduplicate exactly by content hash and near-deduplicate with a published recipe**, either token-set Jaccard at 0.8 and 0.7 (Allamanis) or MinHash with LSH at 0.85 ([The Stack](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.15533)) or over 5-grams at 0.7 ([The Stack v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.19173)), and report the share removed per language. **Add a nearest-neighbour memorisation baseline over `samples/`**, as Allamanis recommends for exposing duplication bias: a high score on the old set and a low one on the new set measures how much of the old score was lookup.

**Split and cap by repository.** Three PLI works already keep repositories on one side of the split ([van Dam & Zaytsev](https://grammarware.net/text/2016/identification.pdf), [Zevin & Holzem](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.07638), [Guesslang](https://guesslang.readthedocs.io/en/latest/)); Nie et al. find that mixed-project results "are always higher" than cross-project or time-segmented ones ([arXiv:2108.09619](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.09619)); Koch et al. segregate donor files between training and test ([arXiv:2407.01529](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.01529)). **Date every file and score each tool on files newer than its data.** Del Bonifro et al. train on a 2017 snapshot and test again on a 2019 one ([arXiv:2101.08508](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.08508)); LiveCodeBench scores each model only on problems released after its cutoff ([arXiv:2403.07974](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07974)); TESSERACT attributes inflated malware-classifier results to temporal and spatial bias ([arXiv:1807.07838](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.07838)). For rule-based tools the relevant date is the Linguist release they embed (go-enry ships v9.5.0 data while Linguist is at 9.7.0); for trained models, the training snapshot; for LLMs, the stated cutoff. Hold the sampling protocol fixed across time slices, because a fresh test set lowers scores by 3% to 15% even without memorisation ([Recht et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10811)).

**Do not assume that "post-cutoff" means unseen, and do not try to decide per file whether a model saw it.** Cao et al. find that models "do not necessarily perform worse on data after the models' cut-off date" ([arXiv:2403.16898](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.16898)); membership-inference attacks "barely outperform random guessing" ([Duan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.07841)). Rely on provenance: first-seen date in the archive plus deduplication. **Keep an exclusion list for learned tools and LLMs** that covers Rosetta Code, Project CodeNet, CodeSearchNet and The Stack in all versions, since identifiers and small LLMs were trained on them (the archives researcher's inference). **Record model identifier, version, query date and prompt for every LLM entry**, as Sallou et al. advise ([arXiv:2312.08055](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.08055)); the model changed during the SCC-GPT study. **Distribute the benchmark as SWHIDs with labels, strata and weights, plus a fetch script.** StarCoder2 releases SWHIDs for its training data ([arXiv:2402.19173](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.19173)), and Jacovi et al. advise against uploading test data in plain text ([arXiv:2305.10160](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.10160)). **Freeze the sampling frame, label protocol and metrics before any tool is run on the new set, and persist raw per-file predictions.** The ACM SIGSOFT benchmarking standard names "tailoring benchmarks specifically for methods being evaluated" as an antipattern ([Benchmarking standard](https://github.com/acmsigsoft/EmpiricalStandards/blob/master/docs/standards/Benchmarking.md)); it applies here because the benchmark's maintainer also maintains Synid, a point the standard does not itself discuss.

### Sampling

**Define the population in one sentence and sample it with known probabilities.** Baltes & Ralph find that in software-engineering research "random sampling is rare" and representativeness is often misunderstood ([arXiv:2002.07764](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.07764)); the repository-mining standard requires a justified selection and lists convenience sampling as an antipattern ([Repository Mining standard](https://github.com/acmsigsoft/EmpiricalStandards/blob/master/docs/standards/RepositoryMining.md)); Kapoor & Narayanan class a test set "not drawn from the distribution of scientific interest" as leakage. Software Heritage offers two documented frames: the most-popular-file-name table over 12.0 billion contents, whose authors note that it "gives more weight to projects which made many changes to the directory containing a given file" ([Lorentz et al.](https://upsilon.cc/zack/research/publications/swh-popular-content-filenames-dataset.pdf)), and The Stack v2 metadata, limited to GitHub origins and default-branch snapshots. Because Software Heritage contents have no intrinsic name, record the name-selection rule and, ideally, all names of each sampled content. **Do not filter by stars; stratify instead.** A threshold of 10 stars keeps precision at 97% for "engineered" projects but recall at 32% ([Munaiah et al.](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1007/s10664-017-9512-6?fields=title,abstract,authors,year,venue)), and most GitHub projects are "personal and inactive" ([Kalliamvakou et al.](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1007/s10664-015-9393-5?fields=title,abstract,authors,year,venue)); every set in group A that filters by popularity inherits that bias.

**Stratify by extension, not by language, with dedicated strata for ambiguous extensions, for names without an extension and for "not code".** Stratifying by language assumes the label; Apache Tika's regression corpus, selected by Tika's own detected type, shows what tool-conditioned sampling looks like ([Tika wiki](https://cwiki-test.apache.org/confluence/x/2iKGBg)). The Berger et al. `.ts` case supports extension strata, and the requester's draft specification already records inclusion probabilities and caps files per origin ([synid-bench](https://github.com/acherm/synid-bench)). Disagreement between The Stack v2's per-file go-enry language, the repository language and the extension is a cheap signal for a "hard" stratum, provided its inclusion probabilities are recorded (the archives researcher's suggestion). **Use more than one forge and add an out-of-distribution second source.** Sallou et al. advise gathering data "from multiple sources"; Magika and PLangRec both name GitHub-only data as a threat; FiFTy falls from 77.5% on its own corpus to 55.9% on a Govdocs1 subset, a figure marked for checking ([FiFTy](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.06148)). **Stratify by file length.** Magika degrades below about 100 bytes and its current model reads 1,024 bytes at each end; Linguist's classifier reads 50 KiB, scc 20,000 bytes; snippet accuracy rises from 77.7% to 84.7% when the minimum length goes from 10 to 100 characters ([Alrashedy thesis](https://dspace.library.uvic.ca/bitstreams/286a2c90-2db5-4187-b522-9d07f4706f09/download)).

### Labelling

**Produce labels without any tool under test and without its extension table.** Magika's authors "specifically avoid using existing content-type detection tools as part of our validation" ([arXiv:2409.13768](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.13768)). **Label each file by humans, double-annotate at least a sample, and publish agreement and an estimated label-error rate.** NLoN reports two raters agreeing on 97% to 98% of lines ([arXiv:1803.07292](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.07292)); Bacchelli et al. size their random samples for 95% confidence and 5% error and report discordant lines ([ICPC 2010](https://www.inf.usi.ch/faculty/lanza/PUBS/P/Bacc2010d.pdf), [ICSE 2012](https://www.si.usi.ch/assets/publications/conf/icse/icse2012/BacchelliSDL12.pdf)); the repository-mining standard asks that manual annotation "assesses inter-rater reliability". The error rate matters because test sets average at least 3.3% label errors, enough to reorder models ([Northcutt et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.14749)), and a few percent is also the size of the differences between top PLI tools (the researchers' observation). **Use parser or compiler acceptance as a second signal, never as the gold label.** PLangRec's grammar check is the model, but its authors had to patch grammars and accept any of five SQL dialects; only 0.31% of sampled C and C++ files in The Stack compile stand-alone ([CompCodeVet](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.06505)); and a parser returns a set of accepting versions, not one label ([Malloy & Power](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/id/eprint/10220/1/JP-Quantifying-2017.pdf)). NapierOne's check that each file opens in its native application is the forensic counterpart ([arXiv:2201.08154](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.08154)). **Use provenance where it exists**: known-language projects, as in Linguist's OCaml corpus ([PR #2227](https://github.com/github/linguist/pull/2227)), or author-declared languages as on online judges; Fetherston & Gollins make the same case for format corpora ([IJDC 2012](https://ijdc.net/index.php/ijdc/article/download/201/270/887)).

**If LLMs help with labelling, measure them first and keep humans as adjudicators.** Ahmed et al. find LLM-human agreement ranging from human level to near zero depending on the task, and suggest model-model agreement above 0.5 as a sign of suitability ([arXiv:2408.05534](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.05534)). One model card already uses an "LLM judge" to verify PLI labels without any human agreement figure ([FrameByFrame](https://huggingface.co/FrameByFrame/programming-language-identification-100plus)). For PLI the hazard is specific: LLMs are also systems under test, and their beliefs partly come from Linguist-labelled corpora, so an LLM-labelled set would favour LLMs and Linguist-family tools (the researchers' inference). **Allow set-valued labels for files valid in several languages, and give composite files a container label or exclude them into a separate stratum.** Linguist's own test whitelists three `.h` files because "a file is fully valid in more than one language"; Zevin & Holzem conclude that "absolute accuracy is unattainable because of the existence of polyglots"; Koch et al. use multi-label truth for format polyglots. **Build "not code" from real, stratified material** (prose, logs, data, configuration, generated and minified files, vendored copies), since Magika's synthetic classes measure separation from noise and the Telegram contest expects OTHER to be "the most common correct answer" ([contest rules](https://contest.com/docs/ML-Competition-2023-r2)). **Version the labels and keep an erratum channel**, one of BetterBench's criteria ([arXiv:2411.12990](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.12990)).

### Taxonomy

**Publish the crosswalk between label sets.** Magika normalised baseline outputs by iterative manual rules "until 99% of the default outputs mapped to a canonical type"; no PLI paper documents such a map. **Label at two levels, exact language and family or parent, and report at both.** Project CodeNet keeps the judge's original language and a mapped one ([CodeNet README](https://github.com/IBM/Project_CodeNet/blob/55c323b527ab3d6510d55edff188bc0a0d7bc5e5/README.md)); FiFTy reports six scenarios at different granularities; Del Bonifro et al. publish confusion groups. Linguist's `group` field covers only 85 of 836 languages and is too idiosyncratic to serve as the hierarchy (the researchers' count and judgement). **Fix dialect granularity before labelling**: Linguist folds Octave into MATLAB but splits SQL into five labels, while Pygments has a separate Octave lexer and no SQL dialect split; Rosetta Code's community debated the same question in 2012 ([Village Pump](https://rosettacode.org/wiki/Rosetta_Code:Village_Pump/Dialects)). **State what "the language of a file" means**, single or multiple, with or without embedded languages; the benchmarking standard requires a discussion of construct validity. **Pin every tool's version and mode.** Linguist replaced naive Bayes with a centroid classifier in v8.0.0 while go-enry and Hyperpolyglot still ship naive Bayes ([Linguist commit](https://github.com/github-linguist/linguist/commit/f7c7baa1191e612043fafe660ed2260d4fd2b4f6)); the same Guesslang filter produced 490,778 or 249,451 items depending on the version ([Jallow & Bugiel](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.16948)); VS Code post-processes Guesslang's scores; Magika has three prediction modes.

### Metrics

**Score every file with its name, without it and with a misleading one.** The OctoLingua post is the only precedent on the same files ([GitHub blog](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/machine-learning/c-or-java-typescript-or-javascript-machine-learning-based-classification-of-programming-languages/)). **Report each tool on its own label set and on the full set**, as Magika does; prefer a tool's declared vocabulary to Magika's empirical rule that a class a tool never emits is unsupported. **Report coverage and accuracy on committed answers separately.** A 2009 MITRE study already separates a tool with "few errors" from its 60% identification rate ([MITRE](https://www.mitre.org/sites/default/files/pdf/10_0004.pdf)); Roussev & Garfinkel prefer "unable to classify" to a false result ([SADFE 2009](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA549423.pdf)); selective classification supplies risk-coverage curves ([Geifman & El-Yaniv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08500)) and the open-set literature an evaluation metric for unknowns ([Dhamija et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.04110)).

**Give two families of numbers from one sample: per-class results and a design-weighted population accuracy.** Magika's authors state that balanced-set metrics are "not directly applicable" to real distributions; Caswell et al. show natural-language identifiers with good benchmark scores reaching about 5% precision in the wild for rare languages ([arXiv:2010.14571](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.14571)); Kossen et al. show how to remove the bias of non-uniform test sampling ([arXiv:2103.05331](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.05331)). **State the macro-F1 formula and report micro, macro and weighted variants**: two formulas are in use and can differ by up to 0.5 ([Opitz & Burst](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.03347)). **Give confidence intervals clustered by repository and compare tools on paired per-file differences** ([Miller](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.00640)); 14 of 24 benchmarks assessed by BetterBench report no statistical significance. **Publish confusion groups and per-stratum results** for each ambiguous extension, length band and time slice. **Ship a datasheet, a one-command replication script, a licence statement and a contact** ([Gebru et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09010); BetterBench), and **plan dated, refreshed slices**, since benchmarks saturate quickly ([Ott et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.04592)) and PLI sets already report 99% and above.

And among the released tools only Magika has a designed "not code" output, while Linguist-family tools express it through a language type or an empty answer and Guesslang has none ([guesslang issue #45](https://github.com/yoeo/guesslang/issues/45)).

## Conclusion

The literature's central weakness is not a shortage of classifiers but of ground truth: almost every published accuracy is agreement between a model and a label that an extension table or Linguist produced. That reframes what a new benchmark contributes. Its lasting value lies in the labelling and sampling protocol, per-file human adjudication with reported agreement on a probability sample with recorded weights, more than in the leaderboard, and it would supply three numbers nobody has: a population-weighted error rate for Linguist-style labels in an archive, the share of files (not of extensions) that sit under an ambiguous extension, and the gap between "seen" and unseen files for each tool family. The same sample would give Software Heritage the first validated estimate of how wrong its extension-based language statistics are.

Two cautions follow from who builds it. Because the benchmark's maintainer also maintains Synid, and because LLMs are both candidates for labelling and systems under test, the protocol should be frozen and published before any tool is run, and labels produced blind to tool output. And because this review itself was cut short, three checks should precede any novelty claim: read the 2025 IEEE Access survey in full, re-run the 2022 to 2026 searches on Google Scholar, DBLP, IEEE Xplore and the ACM Digital Library, and re-read the flagged numbers in the PDFs.
